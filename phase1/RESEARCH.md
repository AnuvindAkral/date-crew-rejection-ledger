## 3. Positioning and what it rules out

The comparison table on S2 is the clearest statement of the brand's strategy:

| Axis | TDC | Everyone else |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Account handling | By expert matchmakers | Through AI or low EQ relationship managers |
| Profile sharing | Hand picked | Random, or algorithm pushed |
| Privacy and control | You decide who sees your profile | Limited privacy and open visibility |
| Time cost | One hour once | 40+ hours a month of swiping |

The page header on S2 reads "No AI. No App. No Endless Swiping."

This is the single most important constraint on the whole exercise. A product that puts a model in the seat of choosing who gets introduced would run straight into the promise the company sells. Any recommendation that leans on AI has to keep the model out of the decision and put it somewhere the brand already tolerates.

**Where the brand already tolerates AI.** S5 states that Magic Link's personal descriptions are "Crafted by TDC's AI to sound warm, engaging, and real", and the same page lists "Smart Profile Insights" with profile views, read time and city-wise interest. So the rule that matters is narrow and useful: AI may draft and summarise what a person already decided. AI may not decide who meets whom. The recommended intervention in DECISIONS.md sits inside that line.

**The company's own framing of the pain.** S15: "By absorbing the search, the mediation, the feedback, and leaving you to focus on just one thing: genuinely connecting with your match." The job to be done is described as absorbing the search work. That points at the matchmaker, not the client, as the person doing the work.

## 4. Current state customer and operational journey

### 4.1 Client journey (verified)

1. Apply through the contact form. S1, S6.
2. Team reviews the profile. Only people they believe they can match are onboarded. S2, S6.
3. Detailed call to understand story, preferences and deal breakers, then the matchmaker explains the process and the client picks a path. S4.
4. Membership path: pay, get a private member dashboard with Magic Link, complete Magic Link, become discoverable to matchmakers. No dedicated matchmaker, no guaranteed intros, no active outreach. S4, S6.
5. Partner Search path: get a dedicated matchmaker, a personalised search strategy, introductions, family handling, post date coaching, custom match reports. S2, S6.
6. Expect 2 to 4 curated introductions a month. S4.
7. Matchmaker touches base every two weeks. S3, S13.
8. Average journey runs 6 to 9 months. S4.
9. A "match" is counted only when both people have met and want a second meeting. Casual chats do not count. S4.

### 4.2 Matchmaker workflow (verified from the job description, S12)

The Matchmaker JD is the most direct public window into how the job actually runs. Under "What You Will Do":

1. **Relationship management.** Main point of contact for a set of premium clients. Understand their life story, relationship patterns, emotional needs. Coach them through doubt, frustration and overwhelm.
2. **Matchmaking and strategy.** Design a match strategy per client. Curate matches from the internal database and external platforms including matrimonial sites and LinkedIn. "Ensure mutual compatibility before suggesting a match, not just profile alignment." "Use both logic and intuition to recommend the right people." "Track client responses, feedback, and patterns over time."
3. **Coaching and emotional support.** Reflect on what is working, offer honest feedback including when a client is being too picky or is self sabotaging.
4. **Coordination and internal systems.** "Manage calendars, follow-ups, notes, and match histories via our CRM." Work with junior matchmakers, sourcing teams and founders. "Document learnings and contribute to matchmaking best practices/playbooks." Protect client privacy.
5. **Brand building.** Social media presence and shareable insights from client journeys.

Three things follow directly from that text. A CRM already exists, so another dashboard is not the answer. Rejection feedback and match histories are supposed to be tracked over time, so the data problem is capture and structure rather than absence. There is a separate sourcing team, so the funnel's first stage is likely produced by sourcing and curated by a matchmaker.

### 4.3 What the public material does not reveal

- Which CRM, and whether it has a rejection reason field at all.
- Whether rejection reasons are stored anywhere beyond free text notes.
- Whether matchmakers share a taxonomy for rejection reasons.
- How many matchmakers are on Partner Search versus Membership, and how cases are assigned.
- Whether a matchmaker manages a client's existing matrimonial profile, which S4 and S11 say TDC does on the client's behalf with authorisation.
- Any funnel period definition, cohort definition, or stage naming convention.
- Whether sourcing volume per client is capped.

These gaps are the reason the experiment in EXPERIMENT.md has to start with capture and definition, not with a model.

## 5. The founder's published theory, and why it is the crux

On 16 September 2026, Oendrila Kapoor published S9. The argument, compressed:

> "I learn very little from what people tell me during the onboarding call. I learn far more from the reasons they reject someone."
>
> "The first conversation is about identity. The rejection conversation is about reality."
>
> "People don't just choose partners. They choose predictions."

S10, published two days later, extends it. A prestigious degree is not intelligence, it is a shortcut that reduces uncertainty about ambition, discipline, earning potential and family approval. "We're often searching for evidence other people will recognize rather than evidence that actually predicts a good partner."

This matters more than a marketing page because it is the company's working hypothesis about its own core workflow, and the supplied scenario data partially contradicts it.

| Founder's claim | Scenario data | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rejection reasons reveal the real preference | 35% of rejections cite reasons already stated in client preferences | For that 35%, the stated preference held. The mismatch is between what was known and what was sent, not between stated and revealed preference |
| Rejections are the richest signal | Some clients reject profiles then later accept profiles with similar characteristics | Rejection is not a stable rule for at least some clients and dimensions |
| People choose predictions, not traits | Not measurable from the supplied data | The 65% of rejections that are not preference related is where this would live |

That leaves a specific and narrow product question. Not "capture preferences better", since the preferences are captured. Not "rank profiles better", since ranking cannot encode a criterion that was never stated. The question is: given that a rejection has already happened and a similar profile is about to be sent again, what should happen at that moment?

The founder's thesis also implies a risk. If rejection reasons are treated as the authoritative expression of client preference, clients who rationalise get their rationalisations codified. The scenario's own data shows some clients accept later what they rejected earlier. So the product has to treat a rejection as a question, not as a rule. That is a constraint, not a feature, and it shapes the recommended intervention.

## 6. Existing products and competitor approaches

### 6.1 Matchmaker tooling already on the market

| Product | Price | Relevant features | Gap against the TDC problem |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SmartMatchApp (S18) | $49, $99 or $149 per admin per month | Smart matchmaking suggestions, custom advanced search, conditional matching workflows, matching scheduler, client-side preference updates, feedback surveys, client activity logs | Treats preferences as fields to filter on. No documented handling of a client whose observed behaviour contradicts their stated preference |
| HoneyBee Match (S19) | $49, $79 or $199 per month | AI profile summary, AI screening summary, AI audio transcription, automated date reminders, automated post date feedback surveys, lead scoring, a cross agency referral marketplace | AI is used to summarise and to score. Feedback is collected as surveys. No documented contradiction detection between rejection history and later acceptances |
| Matchdiary (S20) | Free | Matchmaker CRM, client app, smart matching and compatibility tools, partner preferences, PDF biodata export | Same pattern. Preferences as structured fields, matching as scoring |

### 6.2 Service model comparable

Dating By Blaine (S21) is a US premium matchmaker with a similar operating model: every candidate is screened by hand before being proposed, "not every client accepts every match we propose", and client feedback after each match "directly shapes our search", described as "an iterative process". TDC runs the same loop, in a more instrumented form, with a stated 2 to 4 introductions a month and a 6 to 9 month journey.

### 6.3 Where the differentiation claim sits

Reading those vendor feature pages, none of them publicly documents a mechanism that holds a client's rejection history, the stated preferences, and the later acceptances side by side and surfaces the disagreement at the moment the next profile is chosen. That is an inference from marketing pages, not a proven absence. It is still the most defensible space I found, and it lines up with the JD mandate in S13 on "help structure psychometric or behavioural information" and turning "qualitative information into useful product signals".

The counterpoint is worth stating plainly. TDC is not an unserved market. It runs on a CRM with notes and match histories (S12), it already has AI drafting in Magic Link (S5), it already ships client-facing preference editing (S4), and it already produces "Custom Match Reports and Compatibility Summaries" after every introduction (S2, S6). Whatever gets built has to live inside those surfaces rather than beside them.

## 8. Research limitations

- LinkedIn company page content was read through a search result summary rather than a logged in session. A single post is quoted in S15. Treat the LinkedIn material as indicative.
- The careers board is a Zoho Recruit single page app. The Matchmaker JD in S12 was recovered through a search index that carried most of the body but not all of it. The Product Growth JD in S13 was recovered the same way and is partially truncated. The rubric lines quoted in DECISIONS.md were verified as present.
- No interview, survey, CRM inspection or internal document was available. Every statement about how TDC actually works is either from S12 or S4, or marked as inference.
- Competitor feature claims come from vendor pricing and feature pages. Vendor pages describe intended capability, not realised outcome.
- No client data was used and none was requested.

## 9. What the research changed about the brief

Three findings reshaped the problem framing before any solution work started.

**The first stage of the funnel is not a ranking problem.** The matchmaker JD says to use logic and intuition, and the brand says hand picked over algorithm pushed. The 690 losses at the shared to accepted stage are real, but the ceiling on what a product can recover there is set by what the client has actually articulated, and the supplied 35% figure bounds that.

**The most valuable signal is already being produced and thrown away.** S12 says to track responses, feedback and patterns over time. S9 says rejections are the richest signal in the business. The scenario says that feedback arrives as unstructured free text. So the richest signal exists, is expected to be captured, and arrives unusable. That is a capture and structure problem, not a modelling problem.

**The hiring rubric is unusually specific.** S13 asks a candidate to work out whether a problem should be answered with a process change, an automation, a product or AI, and to find the quickest way to test it. It names funnel drop off analysis, structuring psychometric or behavioural information, and turning qualitative information into product signals. Any brief that answers with "build a ranking model" has already failed the rubric, because S13 itself warns against treating matchmaking as a ranking exercise.
